# CMML Care App — Requirements Discovery

A conversation guide for the first sit-down with Mum and Dad. No technical
decisions here on purpose — the goal is to come away knowing what they
actually need, in their words, before anything gets designed or built.

Nothing in this document is medical advice. The marker lists exist so you know
what to ask about and what to run past the haematology team — the team decides
what actually matters for Dad.

---

## How to run the conversation

A few things that make this kind of session go much better:

- **Ask for stories, not features.** "Walk me through the last transfusion day,
  start to finish" gets you ten times more than "what features do you want?"
  People are bad at specifying software and excellent at describing their week.
- **Ask to see the current system.** There will be one — a notebook, a folder,
  photos on a phone, letters on the fridge, a wall calendar. Ask to see it and
  photograph every page. This is the highest-value artefact you will get all
  morning, and it will tell you more than any answer.
- **Interview both of them, and expect different answers.** Dad is living it;
  Mum is very likely running the logistics. They will care about different
  things and neither is wrong. Where they disagree, write down both.
- **Capture verbatim.** Their words for things ("his numbers", "top-up",
  "the good week") should become the app's words. Don't translate into jargon.
- **Listen hard for what they do NOT want tracked.** Daily symptom logging can
  turn into homework, and watching a number fall can add real anxiety. A thing
  they abandon in two weeks is worse than not building it.
- **Don't design in the room.** Resist "we could make it do X." Just collect.
  You'll build a better thing this afternoon than you would out loud.
- **Watch the energy.** If Dad tires, split it — do the treatment detail with
  Mum and keep the questions to Dad about how he wants to feel using it.

**Ask them to dig out beforehand (or at the start):** the folder of letters,
the last few blood results, the current medicines list, upcoming appointment
letters, any patient-portal logins, and the contact card for the haematology
team.

## 1. Today's reality — open with these

1. Walk me through the last month. What appointments, tests and treatments
   actually happened?
2. Where does all the information live right now? Show me.
3. Can I photograph what you've got?
4. What's the most annoying or stressful part of keeping track of it all?
5. Has there ever been a moment where you couldn't find something you needed,
   or got a number wrong, or missed something?
6. What do you already do that works well and shouldn't change?
7. If this thing only did one job well, what would that job be?

## 2. Who's actually using it

8. Who needs to be able to open this? Both of you? Anyone else?
9. On what — phone, tablet, laptop? Which one is actually within reach when
   you'd need it?
10. Dad, do you want to put things in yourself, or would you rather Mum did it,
    or both of you?
11. If Dad's unwell or in hospital, does Mum need to be able to run all of it
    on her own? What would she need that she doesn't have now?
12. Should us kids be able to look in and see how he's going, without having to
    ring and ask?
13. Is there anything either of you would rather keep private from the other?
    (Ask gently, and be ready for a "no" that means "not in front of him.")
14. Would you ever want to hand a printout, or a link, to a doctor or nurse?

## 3. Blood tests

15. Where do the results come from — hospital lab, community lab, a portal, a
    printed sheet from the nurse, a letter?
16. Do you get a copy every single time? Paper, PDF, or on a screen?
17. Is there a patient portal you already have a login for? What's in it, and
    what's missing from it?
18. How often are bloods done? Does the frequency change — before a transfusion
    vs. routine vs. during a treatment cycle?
19. Which numbers does the team actually talk about at appointments?
20. Which numbers do *you two* watch? Which one makes you feel better or worse
    when you see it?
21. Do you know Dad's usual range, and his trigger points — the number below
    which they transfuse, or above which they'd act?
22. Would you rather type numbers in, photograph the results sheet, or both?
23. How far back do you have results? Do you want the history loaded in, or is
    starting from today fine?
24. Do you want to see every number, or just the handful that matter with the
    rest tucked away?
25. When you look at a result, what's the question in your head? ("Is it up or
    down?" "Is it transfusion time?" "Is it working?")

## 4. Transfusions

26. Red cells, platelets, or both?
27. How often lately? And has that changed over the last few months?
28. What triggers one — a specific number, or how Dad's feeling, or both?
29. Where does it happen, how long does the day take, and does someone stay?
30. Walk me through a transfusion day from leaving the house to getting home.
    (Listen for: bloods/crossmatch beforehand, waiting, cannula, premeds, how
    many units, how long each takes, food, parking, who drives.)
31. What would you want recorded about each one — date, product, units, the
    number before and after, where, how he felt afterwards, any reaction?
32. How long does the lift last? Would it be useful to see that pattern over
    time? (The gap between transfusions is often the clearest single signal of
    how things are trending — worth testing whether that lands for them.)
33. Has there ever been a reaction or a hold-up? Do you want that logged?
34. Has the team mentioned iron levels or ferritin building up from repeated
    transfusions?

## 5. EPO injections

35. Which one, what dose, how often?
36. Who gives it — a nurse, Mum, or Dad himself?
37. At home or at the clinic? How does it get stored and re-supplied?
38. Do you need reminders? Do doses ever get missed or run late?
39. Do you want to track anything about them — site, side effects, aches,
    blood pressure?
40. Has the dose changed over time? Would a record of the changes, and why,
    be useful?
41. How do you know whether it's working? Has the team set a point where
    they'll review that?
42. Are there prescription or funding renewals you need to stay ahead of?

## 6. Other treatment and medicines

43. What else is Dad on — hospital treatment, tablets, injections, supplements?
44. If there are treatment cycles, what does one look like, and where in a cycle
    are you at any given time? Would knowing that at a glance help?
45. Do you have a current medicines list? Is it up to date? Would an always-
    current, shareable one be useful?
46. Anything that changes often, or that's "take as needed"?
47. What side effects are you watching for? Which ones would you want logged?
48. Have transplant, trials or other options been discussed? Do you want
    somewhere to keep those conversations and where they got to?
49. Is anything measured at home — blood pressure, weight, temperature?
50. Anything that has to be timed around treatment — vaccinations, dental work,
    travel?

## 7. Appointments and logistics

51. Who's on the care team, and who do you ring for what?
52. How do appointments arrive — letter, text, phone call, portal?
53. Do you use a calendar already? Which one? Should this feed into it, or
    replace it?
54. What's the usual mix — haematologist, nurse, GP, lab, day unit, scans?
55. What do you need to know *before* you go? (Fasting? Bloods first? Bring the
    meds list? Which entrance? Parking? How long to allow?)
56. What do you want to capture *after* — what was said, what changed, what's
    next?
57. Do you keep a list of questions to ask? Do they get forgotten in the room?
58. Would it help to take notes, or record, during appointments — and would the
    team be OK with that?
59. How far is the travel? Is getting there a burden worth planning around?

## 8. How he's actually doing, day to day

60. Between appointments, how do you judge whether Dad's doing well?
61. What are the signs things are heading downhill? (Energy, breathlessness,
    appetite, sleep, bruising, sweats, temperature, mood.)
62. Would a quick check-in — daily or weekly — be useful, or would it feel like
    homework? Be honest.
63. If useful: how quick does it have to be to actually happen? Three taps? A
    number out of ten?
64. Would it help to be able to say "he was flat all week" and see that sitting
    next to the blood results?
65. Fatigue is usually the big one. How would you describe a good day versus a
    bad day, in your own words?
66. Is there anything you'd rather *not* be looking at on a screen every day?

## 9. Progress over time

67. When you say "progress" — what would tell you he's doing well?
68. Charts, plain numbers, or just better / same / worse?
69. Over what window — since the last appointment, six months, since diagnosis?
70. Would you want a one-page summary to take to the haematologist? What would
    be on it?
71. What would you want to be able to answer instantly when a doctor asks?
    ("When was the last transfusion?" "What was his haemoglobin in June?")
72. Do you want to see a downward trend clearly, or does that add worry you'd
    rather not have? What's the right way to show a bad stretch?

## 10. Documents and letters

73. Where do letters, discharge summaries, results and scan reports go now?
74. Would you want to photograph and file them, and find them again by date or
    type?
75. What else is worth keeping in one place — care team contact card, NHI
    number, hospital record numbers, insurance, advance care planning or
    enduring power of attorney documents?
76. What would you want to be able to hand someone in an emergency?

## 11. Reminders and red flags

77. What gets forgotten at the moment?
78. Do you want reminders? For what, and how — a notification, an email, both?
79. Should Mum be reminded as well as Dad?
80. Has the team given you specific red-flag instructions — a temperature to
    ring about, symptoms that mean go straight in? Should those be one tap
    away, with the number to call?
81. Where should emergency and after-hours contacts live?
82. Important one: do you want the app itself to flag things — "his haemoglobin
    is dropping"? Or is interpreting the numbers the clinic's job and the app
    should just show them clearly? (My strong lean is the latter: show, don't
    interpret. Worth hearing what they expect, though.)

## 12. The care team

83. Would you tell the haematology team you're doing this? Would you want their
    input on what to track?
84. Have they already asked you to keep a record of anything?
85. Would they accept a summary or printout from you? In what form?
86. Is there a hospital system or portal that already does part of this — and
    what does it not do?

## 13. Boundaries and how it should feel

87. Is there anything about tracking all of this that feels uncomfortable?
88. Dad, do you want to see all of it, or would you rather Mum held the detail
    and you saw the simple version?
89. Is there a version of this that would make things worse rather than better?
    What would that look like?
90. Should it feel clinical and precise, or calm and warm?
91. Would you want anything in it that isn't medical — photos, good days, what
    he managed that week, milestones?

## 14. Practical constraints

92. Is the internet at home reliable? Is there phone signal at the hospital?
93. Any eyesight or dexterity things to design around — text size, buttons,
    screen brightness?
94. Honestly, how comfortable is each of you with apps? What's a thing you both
    already use happily?
95. Does it need to work with no signal — in a hospital basement, or in the
    day unit?
96. On both your own phones, or one shared device?
97. If a phone got lost or broken, how much would it hurt to lose this?
98. Who else should be able to get into it if something happened to both of you?
99. Are you comfortable with this living online at all, or should it stay on
    your own devices?

## 15. Closing

100. If I built this and you'd stopped using it two weeks later — what would the
     reason have been?
101. What's the first thing you'd want to see when you open it?
102. Is there anything I haven't asked about that's been on your mind?

## Key markers — the checklist to make sure nothing's missed

Use this to prompt them ("does the team talk about any of these?") and to run
past the haematology nurse. Don't present it as a list of things to start
measuring — it's a net for catching anything they'd otherwise forget to
mention. Tick what's relevant to Dad; ignore the rest.

### Blood counts (the routine full blood count)

- Haemoglobin — the anaemia and transfusion driver, and probably the number
  they already watch
- White cell count
- Monocytes (absolute count) — the defining feature of CMML, and usually one
  the haematologist tracks closely
- Neutrophils — infection risk
- Platelets — bruising and bleeding risk
- Blasts / blood film comments — relevant to how the disease is classified
- MCV and reticulocytes — the character of the anaemia and the marrow's response

### Chemistry and supportive bloods

- Ferritin and iron studies — iron loading from repeated transfusions
- LDH
- Kidney function (creatinine / eGFR)
- Liver function
- B12, folate, iron — other treatable contributors to anaemia
- Serum erythropoietin level — relevant to whether EPO injections are likely
  to work
- CRP — infection

### Disease and staging (occasional, but worth a home)

- Bone marrow biopsy dates and findings
- Cytogenetics / karyotype results
- Molecular or gene panel results
- The subtype and stage the team uses for Dad
- Any risk score they've mentioned
- Spleen size, if it gets noted at examination or on scans

### Treatment record

- **Transfusions:** date, product (red cells / platelets), number of units,
  haemoglobin before and after, where, any reaction
- **Interval between transfusions** — the trend here is often the most
  meaningful "how are we going" signal
- **EPO:** product, dose, date, who gave it, site, missed doses, dose changes
  and the reason
- **Other therapy:** what, cycle and day, dose, any delays or reductions and why
- Iron chelation, if that ever starts
- Antibiotics, steroids, growth factors
- The current full medicines list

### Symptoms and wellbeing

- Fatigue
- Breathlessness / how far he can walk today
- Temperature (fevers matter urgently in this disease — worth knowing the
  team's exact instruction)
- Bruising, bleeding, mouth ulcers, infections
- Appetite and weight
- Night sweats
- Sleep and mood
- A simple good day / bad day marker

### Admin

- Appointments: who, where, when, what to bring, what came out of it, next step
- Care team contacts and after-hours numbers
- NHI and hospital record numbers
- Letters and documents
- The running list of questions to ask next time

## Questions worth putting to the haematology team

Either at the next appointment or via the nurse:

- Which markers would you actually want us watching between visits?
- What are Dad's specific numbers — the transfusion threshold, the temperature
  to ring about, anything that means come straight in?
- Is there a portal or record we should be using rather than duplicating?
- Would a one-page summary from us be useful to you? In what format?
- When will you review whether the EPO is working, and what would that look
  like?
- Is there anything you'd like us to keep a record of that we currently aren't?

## Notes to self — don't decide these in the room

- **Show, don't interpret.** The app displays what's recorded and what the team
  has said. It should not calculate risk, suggest thresholds, or imply a
  clinical conclusion.
- **Health data is sensitive.** Where it's stored, who can reach it, and what
  happens when a device is lost are real decisions, not afterthoughts.
- **Manual entry has to be almost free.** If logging a transfusion takes more
  than a few taps, it won't happen for long.
- **Losing the data must be survivable.** Export and backup from day one.
- **The current system is the spec.** Whatever the notebook or the folder does
  well, the app has to do at least as well, or it'll get abandoned.
